# Materi A · Route 1 · Levels 1 and 2

:::note How Materi A is used
About 60 minutes, five cards. Levels 1 and 2: describe the emotional effect of an unfamiliar health platform, rate three designs of a career platform between warmth and credibility, then analyse a health-prevention portal, develop four measures, justify them and decide on the first 12 weeks. The cards give you the ideas and the rules you use in Task 1.
:::

## A1 · Emotions steer behaviour: attention, motivation, trust, memory and decisions · 12 min · Core

> People feel before they judge, and the feeling steers attention, motivation, trust, memory and decisions. Emotional bonding is the result over time: the sense that a product is on your side.

:::box In plain words
**The idea.** People feel before they judge. Within moments a screen makes them feel welcome or distant, safe or unsure, and that feeling steers what they do next: where they look (attention), whether they go on (motivation), whether they believe what they read (trust), what they remember afterwards (memory) and what they choose (decisions). Psychology and neuro-design study these effects. Emotional bonding is the result over time: the sense that a product is on your side, which makes people come back.
**Why it matters.** Block 1.1 asks for the spontaneous emotional effect of WellTrack on a first-time user and for effect statements of the form “for users this could mean that …”. This card gives you the five things a feeling acts on, so you can say what it does.
**How to read the picture.** The same Moss & Pine order confirmation twice: cold and warm.
:::

![The same Moss & Pine order confirmation, cold and warm. Shown here in its starting state; in the Neuro-UX Lab every version and every stress test can be used.](_figures/d7_A1_0.png)

**What a feeling acts on**

| Acts on | What it means | In an interface |
|---|---|---|
| Attention | What people notice and what they miss. | A worried person reads the warning, not the offer. |
| Motivation | Whether they go on or stop. | A sense of progress keeps people in a long form. |
| Trust | Whether they believe what they read. | A calm, clear confirmation is believed. |
| Memory | What stays after the visit. | The end of a visit colours the whole of it. |
| Decisions | What they choose next. | Uncertainty makes people postpone or go elsewhere. |

:::note Case assumption
Moss & Pine, Hana, Jonas, Wanda and Bruno are made up for this example. The three levels of emotional design follow Norman (2004); emotion as part of reasoning follows Damasio (1994); the peak-end rule follows Kahneman et al. (1993).
:::

:::rules How to decide when this comes up in the task
- Describe the first feeling a screen gives, before you judge whether it works.
- Ask what the feeling does: where people look, whether they go on, whether they believe it, what they remember, what they choose.
- Write an effect as a hypothesis: “for users this could mean that …”.
- Look hardest at the strongest moment and at the end of a visit: people remember those.
- A correct product that feels distant is used once; a product that feels on your side is used again.
:::

:::note Extra · go deeper (optional reading)
Norman (2004) describes three levels on which a product acts. The visceral level is the immediate reaction to how it looks. The behavioural level is how it feels to use: whether it works, responds and lets you stay in control. The reflective level is what it means to you afterwards, what you tell others about it. A product can win one level and lose another: beautiful at first sight, frustrating to use, and remembered as frustrating.
:::

^src: Norman 2004 · Damasio 1994 · Kahneman et al. 1993

## A2 · Usable is not enough: the emotional factors of an experience · 12 min · Core

> Usable is not the same as a good experience. Safety, control, joy, belonging and recognition, and their opposites, frustration and uncertainty, decide how a usable product feels.

:::box In plain words
**The idea.** A product can be usable and still a poor experience. Usability asks whether people can reach their goal; emotional experience quality asks how it feels on the way and afterwards. The emotional factors that matter most in UX are safety (I know what is happening), control (I can steer and undo), joy (something is pleasant), belonging (this is for people like me), recognition (my effort is seen), and their opposites: frustration and uncertainty. A screen can pass every usability check and still leave people unsure, unseen or frustrated.
**Why it matters.** Block 1.1 asks you to sort WellTrack's observations into trust, safety, motivation, frustration, closeness and experience quality, Block 1.2 asks you to rate three designs on trust, motivation, safety, clarity and experience quality, and Block 2.1 asks you to sort LifeSupport's problems into five such categories. This card gives you the factors to name what you see.
**How to read the picture.** One Moss & Pine checkout with three switches: safety, control and recognition.
:::

![A Moss & Pine checkout step: safety, control and recognition. Shown here in its starting state; in the Neuro-UX Lab every version and every stress test can be used.](_figures/d7_A2_0.png)

**The emotional factors of UX**

| Factor | What people feel | A sign it is missing |
|---|---|---|
| Safety | I know what is happening. | “Did it work?” |
| Control | I can steer and undo. | No way back from a step. |
| Joy | Something is pleasant. | Every step feels like a chore. |
| Belonging | This is for people like me. | Words and pictures for someone else. |
| Recognition | My effort is seen. | A finished task ends in silence. |
| Frustration and uncertainty | I am stuck, or I do not know. | Errors that blame; waiting without a word. |

:::note Case assumption
Moss & Pine, Hana, Jonas, Wanda and Bruno are made up for this example. Experience beyond usability follows Hassenzahl (2010); user experience as perceptions and responses before, during and after use follows ISO 9241-210.
:::

:::rules How to decide when this comes up in the task
- First check usability: can people reach their goal? Then ask how it feels on the way.
- Safety: do people know what is happening and what comes next?
- Control: can they steer, change and undo?
- Recognition and belonging: does the product see their effort and feel made for people like them?
- Frustration and uncertainty: where do they get stuck or wonder whether something worked?
:::

:::note Extra · go deeper (optional reading)
Hassenzahl (2010) separates what a product does (its pragmatic quality: useful, usable) from what it means to people (its hedonic quality: stimulation, identification). Both shape the overall experience. A health or career product that is only pragmatic is used when people must; one that also gives a sense of competence and being understood is used when they could choose otherwise.
:::

^src: Hassenzahl 2010 · Norman 2004 · ISO 9241-210

## A3 · Where feelings come from: visual mood, interaction, tone and feedback · 12 min · Core

> Feelings come from four things on the screen: visual mood, interaction patterns, tone and feedback. Most of the effect sits in feedback and tone at critical moments: an error, a wait, a success.

:::box In plain words
**The idea.** Feelings in a product are not added by a campaign; they come from four things on the screen. Visual mood: colour, images, space and type set a temperature before anything is read. Interaction patterns: what happens when people act, how fast, and whether they can undo. Tone: the words, above all in microcopy, error messages and confirmations. Feedback: whether the product says what happened, what comes next and that it went well. Most of the emotional effect sits in feedback and tone at the critical moments: an error, a wait, a success.
**Why it matters.** Block 1.1 asks about WellTrack's technical errors and missing positive feedback, Block 1.2 asks about CareerPath's unclear feedback, and Block 2.1 asks about LifeSupport's distant visual language, technical feedback and correct but cold tone. This card gives you the four sources to say where a feeling comes from.
**How to read the picture.** One Moss & Pine error message written three ways: technical, cheerful and supportive.
:::

![One Moss & Pine payment error: technical, cheerful and supportive. Shown here in its starting state; in the Neuro-UX Lab every version and every stress test can be used.](_figures/d7_A3_0.png)

**Four sources of emotional effect**

| Source | What it sets | A question to ask |
|---|---|---|
| Visual mood | A temperature before reading: warm or distant, calm or loud. | Does the look fit what people come here for? |
| Interaction patterns | Whether people feel in control. | Is every step reversible and responsive? |
| Tone | Whether the product sounds like a person on their side. | Would a helpful person say it this way? |
| Feedback | Safety and recognition after each action. | Does it say what happened, what comes next, and that it went well? |

:::note Case assumption
Moss & Pine, Hana, Jonas, Wanda and Bruno are made up for this example. Tone and personality in interfaces follow Walter (2011); visibility of system status and helpful error messages are Nielsen's heuristics (1994); feedback as a basic design principle follows Norman (2013).
:::

:::rules How to decide when this comes up in the task
- Visual mood: what temperature do colour, images, space and type set before anything is read?
- Interaction: what happens when people act, and can they undo it?
- Tone: read the error messages and confirmations aloud; would a person say that?
- Feedback: after every action, does the product say what happened and what comes next?
- Celebrate real successes calmly; a finished task that ends in silence wastes a moment of recognition.
:::

:::note Extra · go deeper (optional reading)
A supportive error message has four parts: what happened, in plain words; what it means for the person (was anything lost or charged?); what they can do now; and where help is. It never blames, never hides behind a code, and never jokes about a problem the person feels. The same four parts work for waiting: what is happening, how long it may take, what the person can do meanwhile.
:::

^src: Walter 2011 · Norman 2013 · Nielsen 1994

## A4 · Activation, overload and manipulation: when emotion turns against use · 12 min · Core

> Activation is not bonding. Many stimuli can overload and push; more feeling than the situation carries is over-emotionalisation; a feeling that does not fit is incongruent; emotion used against people is manipulation.

:::box In plain words
**The idea.** Emotion can activate people for a moment or bond them for years, and the two are not the same. Many stimuli, badges, countdowns and exclamation marks raise activation, but they can overload, tire and feel pushy. Over-emotionalisation is more feeling than the situation carries; incongruent address is a feeling that does not fit it, such as cheerfulness about a worry. Manipulation, often called dark patterns, uses emotion against people's own interest: false urgency, guilt, hidden costs. Lasting use comes from feelings that fit the situation and keep their promise.
**Why it matters.** Block 1.1 asks for WellTrack's two biggest emotional weaknesses, Block 1.2 asks you to judge CareerPath's Variant A with many emotional stimuli and to name the conflicts between emotionalisation and credibility, and Block 2.1 warns against measures that are only decoration. This card gives you the line between activation and manipulation.
**How to read the picture.** The matrix places five Moss & Pine messages by how strongly they activate (upwards) and how well they fit the situation (to the right). A position is a reading of the example, not a score.
:::

![Five Moss & Pine messages by how strongly they activate and how well they fit the situation · Exploratory.](_figures/d7_A4_0.png)

**Where emotion turns against use**

| Risk | What it is | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Overload | Too many emotional stimuli at once. | People tire and miss what matters. |
| Over-emotionalisation | More feeling than the situation carries. | It feels exaggerated and less credible. |
| Incongruent address | A feeling that does not fit the situation. | People feel not taken seriously. |
| Manipulation | Emotion used against people's interest: false urgency, guilt, hidden costs. | Short-term clicks, long-term loss of trust. |

:::note Case assumption
Moss & Pine, Hana, Jonas, Wanda and Bruno are made up for this example. False urgency, scarcity and confirmshaming as dark patterns follow Mathur et al. (2019); the ethics of persuasive technology follow Fogg (2003).
:::

:::rules How to decide when this comes up in the task
- Count the stimuli on a screen: badges, banners, countdowns, exclamation marks. Do they help the task or compete with it?
- Ask whether the feeling fits the situation: a worry needs calm, a success may be celebrated.
- Check every urgency or scarcity message: is it true?
- Ask whom the emotion serves: the user's goal, or only the company's?
- Prefer feelings that keep their promise; they build lasting use.
:::

:::note Extra · go deeper (optional reading)
A simple test for manipulation: would the message still work if the person knew exactly why it was written this way? A care reminder still works when people know it is meant to keep their plants alive. A countdown that resets every visit stops working the moment people see through it, and takes their trust in the next message with it.
:::

^src: Mathur et al. 2019 · Fogg 2003 · Walter 2011

## A5 · Trust and perceived competence, and weighing a measure · 12 min · Core

> Trust links thinking and feeling. People judge competence, honesty and goodwill from small signals. Weigh measures on effect on users, feasibility, risk and time pressure, with a reason.

:::box In plain words
**The idea.** Trust is where thinking and feeling meet. People trust a product when it seems competent (it knows what it is doing), honest (it says what will happen) and benevolent (it is on their side), and they judge all three from small signals: a clear confirmation, an error message that does not blame, information that says where it comes from. A beautiful design raises perceived competence at first; broken promises lower it fast. To put measures in order, weigh each on the four questions Block 2.1 names: effect on users, feasibility, risk and time pressure.
**Why it matters.** Block 1.1 asks about WellTrack's health information that does not build trust, Block 1.2 asks you to rate trust in three designs and choose one, and Block 2.1 asks for four measures, their justification on these four criteria and a decision for 12 weeks. The example uses Moss & Pine and its own measures, so the answer for the task cases is not given.
**How to read the picture.** First the chain from a small signal to trust and use. Then three measures Moss & Pine considered, rated on effect on users, feasibility, risk and time pressure.
:::

![From a small signal to trust and use.](_figures/d7_A5_0.png)

![Moss & Pine weighs three measures on effect on users, feasibility, risk and time pressure.](_figures/d7_A5_1.png)

:::note Case assumption
Moss & Pine, Hana, Jonas, Wanda and Bruno are made up for this example. Credibility and perceived competence follow Fogg (2003); first impressions within a fraction of a second follow Lindgaard et al. (2006); rating options on the same criteria with a reason follows Gibbons (2018). The four criteria are the ones the plan names for the case study.
:::

:::rules How to decide when this comes up in the task
- Look for the small signals of competence: clear feedback, careful wording, information that says where it comes from.
- Look for signals of honesty: no surprises, no hidden conditions, errors that say what happened.
- A good look raises trust at first; a broken promise lowers it fast.
- Rate every measure on the same four questions: effect on users, feasibility, risk, time pressure.
- A rating is a judgement with a reason, never a mark.
:::

:::note Extra · go deeper (optional reading)
Fogg (2003) found that people judge the credibility of a website largely from surface signals: design, clarity, small errors. This is why a typo or a technical error message can cost more trust than its size suggests, and why careful wording in confirmations and errors is one of the cheapest ways to build it.
:::

^src: Fogg 2003 · Lindgaard et al. 2006 · Gibbons 2018

## B1 · Emotional UX as part of the UX architecture, not a campaign · 20 min · Core

> What people feel in a product comes mostly from feedback, tone and interaction, which teams build every day. Emotional UX belongs in the UX architecture, with a vision, principles, a tone guide, patterns and an owner.

:::box In plain words
**The idea.** In many companies emotion is treated as a marketing question: a campaign, a slogan, a nicer picture. But what people feel in a product comes mostly from its feedback, its tone in critical moments and its interaction logic, which product teams build every day. Emotional UX therefore belongs in the UX architecture: principles for how every touchpoint should feel, a tone guide, patterns for confirmations, errors and waiting, and someone who owns them. At senior level it becomes a leadership task when it shapes loyalty, acceptance and how the company is perceived.
**Why it matters.** Block 3.1 asks for a guiding vision for emotional UX, three decisions on trust, emotional safety, motivation and supportive interaction, and an order of which touchpoints, interactions or communication patterns come first, for a company that sees emotion as marketing. You decide the principles, not the pictures.
**How to read the picture.** Left Moss & Pine treating emotion as a campaign, right building it into the UX architecture. Below, the layers from a guiding vision to every message.
:::

![Moss & Pine treating emotion as a campaign, or building it into the UX architecture.](_figures/d7_B1_0.png)

![The layers of emotional UX, from a guiding vision to every message.](_figures/d7_B1_1.png)

:::note Case assumption
Moss & Pine, Hana, Jonas, Wanda and Bruno are made up for this example. Emotion as a property of the product follows Norman (2004) and Hassenzahl (2010); tone and personality as a design system of their own follow Walter (2011).
:::

:::rules How to decide when this comes up in the task
- Check where emotion is decided today: by marketing in campaigns, or in the product's feedback and tone?
- Write a guiding vision that says how people should feel in every touchpoint, including when something goes wrong.
- Turn it into a few principles and a tone guide every team uses.
- Build patterns once for the critical moments: confirmations, errors, waiting.
- At senior level, emotional UX is a leadership task when it shapes loyalty, acceptance and how the company is seen.
:::

:::note Extra · go deeper (optional reading)
A guiding vision for emotional UX is short and testable: one sentence that names the feeling and covers the hard moments. “Customers feel looked after and sure of what happens, also when something goes wrong” can be checked on every screen. “We delight our customers” cannot, and it invites over-emotionalisation.
:::

^src: Norman 2004 · Walter 2011 · Hassenzahl 2010

## B2 · Seriousness against closeness: the conflicts of emotional UX · 20 min · Core

> Seriousness against closeness, activation against credibility, brand effect against user effect. Over-emotionalisation and manipulation are strategic risks. Fit the feeling to the situation and say what each choice gives up.

:::box In plain words
**The idea.** Emotional UX sits between goals that pull against each other: seriousness against closeness (a brand that must be credible and approachable), activation against credibility (more stimuli against looking like a sales channel), brand effect against user effect (what marketing wants to say against what a worried user needs to hear), and speed against care. Over-emotionalisation and manipulation are not only design faults but strategic risks: they weaken credibility and acceptance. The way through is to fit the feeling to the situation, write the rule down, and say what each choice gives up.
**Why it matters.** Block 3.1 asks for three guiding decisions, a risk analysis of wrong assumptions about emotional effect, credibility and user behaviour, and a governance model for conflicts between brand, expertise and user effect. Each of these is a decision between conflicting goals.
**How to read the picture.** The same Moss & Pine reminder for four situations, with the feeling each one needs.
:::

![The Moss & Pine message tool in four situations. Shown here in its starting state; in the Neuro-UX Lab every version and every stress test can be used.](_figures/d7_B2_0.png)

**Standing conflicts of emotional UX**

| Conflict | Who usually stands for each side | What to ask |
|---|---|---|
| Seriousness against closeness | The experts, against marketing and the brand. | Can it be warm and correct at once? |
| Activation against credibility | Marketing's targets, against long-term trust. | Would the message still work if people knew why it was written? |
| Brand effect against user effect | What the company wants to say, against what the person needs now. | What does this person need in this situation? |

:::note Case assumption
Moss & Pine, Hana, Jonas, Wanda and Bruno are made up for this example. The four situations are a reading of the example, not a rule for every product.
:::

:::rules How to decide when this comes up in the task
- Name the conflict as two goals and say who stands for each: marketing, the experts, the product team, the users.
- Decide the feeling by the situation: offer, care, delay, loss; not by the brand alone.
- Treat every false urgency or inappropriate cheerfulness as a risk to credibility, not as a style question.
- Write the rule down, so the conflict is not fought again on every message.
- Say what the choice gives up; a decision that gives up nothing has not been made.
:::

:::note Extra · go deeper (optional reading)
Over-emotionalisation is often a symptom of a conflict that was never settled: marketing adds warmth to every message because no one decided where warmth belongs. A short matrix in the tone guide (which feeling for which situation) settles it once and gives every team the same answer.
:::

^src: Mathur et al. 2019 · Fogg 2003 · Gibbons 2018

## B3 · Who owns emotional UX, and deciding with little data on feelings · 20 min · Core

> Emotional quality lives only with governance: an owner of the principles and the tone guide, experts who check correctness, marketing who owns the brand, and a rule for conflicts. A decision with little data says what it decides, does not know, would reverse and gives up.

:::box In plain words
**The idea.** Emotional quality lives only with governance: someone who owns the principles and the tone guide, the experts who check that a warm message is still correct, marketing who owns the brand, and a rule for when they disagree. Data on feelings is rarely complete, so a decision under uncertainty says what you decide, what you do not know, when you would reverse it and what you give up. Small, reversible changes to words and feedback are the safest place to start, because they can be checked soon.
**Why it matters.** Block 3.1 asks for a governance model for emotional UX principles, a decision despite patchy data, and a statement to the management. This card gives you the parts of each.
**How to read the picture.** The four boxes at the top are the parts of a decision, filled in for Moss & Pine. The two boxes below are the rule for future decisions.
:::

![A decision under uncertainty, filled in for Moss & Pine.](_figures/d7_B3_0.png)

**Three parts of emotional UX governance**

| Part | The question | An example (Moss & Pine) |
|---|---|---|
| Who owns what | Who owns the principles and the tone guide; who uses them? | The head of customer experience owns them; every team uses them. |
| Who checks | Who makes sure warmth stays correct? | The nursery checks care advice; marketing checks the brand. |
| How a conflict is decided | What happens when brand, expertise and user effect disagree? | Correctness, then the customer, then the brand; the product owner settles a tie. |

:::note Case assumption
Moss & Pine, Hana, Jonas, Wanda and Bruno are made up for this example. Deciding with limited information follows Simon (1956); the premortem follows Klein (2007).
:::

:::rules How to decide when this comes up in the task
- Name who owns the emotional UX principles and the tone guide, and who only uses them.
- Give the experts a veto on correctness, and marketing ownership of campaigns.
- Settle conflicts by correctness first, then the effect on the person in that situation, then the brand.
- Say what you decide, what you do not know, when you would reverse it and what you give up.
- Start where changes are cheap, quick to check and easy to reverse: words and feedback.
:::

:::note Extra · go deeper (optional reading)
Imagine it is a year later and the new tone has failed, and ask why (Klein 2007). The usual answers: it sounded friendly but stopped being precise, so the experts withdrew; marketing used it for urgency; each team bent it until there were five tones again. Each answer is a governance gap, not a writing gap.
:::

^src: Simon 1956 · Klein 2007 · Walter 2011
