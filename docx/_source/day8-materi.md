# Materi A · Route 1 · Levels 1 and 2

:::note How Materi A is used
About 60 minutes, five cards. Levels 1 and 2: describe the narrative gaps of an unfamiliar start-up platform, rate three journeys of a further-training platform between efficiency and dramaturgy, then analyse an onboarding flow, develop four measures, justify them and decide on the first 12 weeks. The cards give you the ideas and the rules you use in Task 1.
:::

## A1 · Why people experience a process as a story · 12 min · Core

> People make sense of what they do as a story: where they start, where they are going, how far they have come. A process that shows only functions still gets read as a journey, and without a story it feels lost or endless.

:::box In plain words
**The idea.** People make sense of what they do as a story: where they started, where they want to go, what stands in the way and how far they have come. A digital process is experienced the same way, whether it was designed as a story or not. When it shows only functions and forms, people still look for the story, and when they cannot find one they feel lost, bored or left alone. Storytelling in UX/UI design means building the journey so that the story people look for is there: a beginning that shows the goal, a way that shows progress, and an end that feels like an end.
**Why it matters.** Block 1.1 asks for the effect of StartSmart on a first-time user and for effect statements of the form “for users this could mean that …”, and Block 2.1 asks why GreenHome's complete onboarding is abandoned. This card gives you the reason a working process can still feel like no journey at all.
**How to read the picture.** The same Lantern Kitchens planner twice: as a list of functions and as a journey.
:::

![The same Lantern Kitchens planner as a list of functions and as a journey. Shown here in its starting state; in the Neuro-UX Lab every version and every stress test can be used.](_figures/d8_A1_0.png)

**Processing a process as a story**

| People look for | What they ask | If it is missing |
|---|---|---|
| A beginning | Where do I start, and is this for me? | They browse and leave. |
| A goal | What will I have at the end? | No reason to begin or to go on. |
| A way | How far have I come, how much is left? | It feels endless; they give up halfway. |
| An end | Am I done, and was it worth it? | They are unsure it worked. |

:::note Case assumption
Lantern Kitchens, Iris, Pavel, June and Arne are made up for this example. Narrative as a way of making sense of experience follows Bruner (1991); storytelling for user experience follows Quesenbery and Brooks (2010); design as a sequence with a beginning, tension and resolution follows Lupton (2017).
:::

:::rules How to decide when this comes up in the task
- Look at the first screen and ask: does it show where the journey leads, or only what the product can do?
- Ask whether people can tell, at any point, where they are and how much is left.
- Ask whether the end of the journey feels like an end.
- Write an effect as a hypothesis: “for users this could mean that …”.
- A process that works but tells no story is still experienced as a journey: a poor one.
:::

:::note Extra · go deeper (optional reading)
Bruner (1991) argued that people do not store experience as a list of facts but as stories with actors, goals and turns. In a digital product the actor is the user, the goal is what they came to do, and the turns are the steps, the obstacles and the help. A product that does not offer this structure leaves people to build it themselves, and many give up before they do.
:::

^src: Bruner 1991 · Quesenbery & Brooks 2010 · Lupton 2017

## A2 · The elements of narrative guidance: from starting point to resolution · 12 min · Core

> Seven elements guide a journey: starting situation, goal, relevance, obstacle, support, progress and resolution. Without a visible goal and progress it feels endless; without a resolution it ends without achievement.

:::box In plain words
**The idea.** Narrative guidance has seven elements. The starting situation: where the user is now. The goal: what they will have at the end. Relevance: why it matters for them. The obstacle: what makes it hard, said honestly. Support: what helps them over it. Progress: how far they have come. The resolution: the end, where the goal is reached and seen. A process that leaves out the goal or the progress feels endless; one that leaves out the resolution ends without a sense of achievement.
**Why it matters.** Block 1.1 asks for five places where StartSmart's narrative guidance is missing and for its two biggest weaknesses, and Block 2.1 asks for GreenHome's central narrative deficits. This card gives you the seven elements to say which part of the journey is missing.
**How to read the picture.** One Lantern Kitchens planner with three switches: a goal, visible progress and a resolution.
:::

![The Lantern Kitchens planner: goal, progress and resolution. Shown here in its starting state; in the Neuro-UX Lab every version and every stress test can be used.](_figures/d8_A2_0.png)

**The seven elements of narrative guidance**

| Element | In a story | In a digital journey |
|---|---|---|
| Starting situation | Where the hero is. | “You have a room and an idea.” |
| Goal | What the hero wants. | “At the end you have a plan and an offer.” |
| Relevance | Why it matters. | “So your cabinets fit to the centimetre.” |
| Obstacle | What stands in the way. | “Measuring is the hardest part.” |
| Support | Who helps. | A short guide right at that step. |
| Progress | How far the hero has come. | A path of stages with “you are here”. |
| Resolution | The goal reached. | The plan shown, the next step named. |

:::note Case assumption
Lantern Kitchens, Iris, Pavel, June and Arne are made up for this example. The dramatic arc follows Freytag (1863); people speeding up as they see a goal come closer follows Kivetz et al. (2006).
:::

:::rules How to decide when this comes up in the task
- Starting situation: does the journey begin where the user is, in their words?
- Goal and relevance: does it say what they will have and why it matters for them?
- Obstacle and support: does it say honestly what is hard and offer help at that point?
- Progress: can they see how far they have come?
- Resolution: does the end show that the goal is reached?
:::

:::note Extra · go deeper (optional reading)
Kivetz et al. (2006) showed the goal-gradient effect: people work faster the closer they see themselves to a goal, and even an illusion of progress (a card that starts with two stamps already filled) makes them finish more often. In a journey this is why visible progress matters most in the middle, where people are furthest from both the start and the end and most likely to stop.
:::

^src: Quesenbery & Brooks 2010 · Freytag 1863 · Kivetz et al. 2006

## A3 · Telling a story or guiding with one: dramaturgy in a journey · 12 min · Core

> Telling a story is not guiding with one. Dramaturgy in a journey is tension, expectation, sequence, transition, confirmation and emotional context, and it lives in small places: the first screen, the line between steps, the message after a step.

:::box In plain words
**The idea.** Telling a story about a product (a video, a slogan, a brand story) is not the same as guiding people with a story. Narratively structured guidance builds the journey itself: tension (something is at stake), expectation (people know what comes next), sequence (the steps follow a logic people can feel, not an administrative one), transition (each step leads to the next), confirmation (a finished step is acknowledged) and emotional context (the step is framed by what it means for them). Most of it lives in small places: the first screen, the line between two steps, the message after a step is done.
**Why it matters.** Block 1.1 asks about StartSmart's missing transitions and steps that are not picked up, Block 1.2 asks you to judge a strongly narrative journey against an efficient one, and Block 2.1 asks about GreenHome's administrative sequence and transitions. This card gives you the dramaturgical means to name them.
**How to read the picture.** The moment between two steps of the Lantern Kitchens planner, written three ways: a bare Next, a staged scene and a short transition.
:::

![The moment between two Lantern Kitchens steps: bare, staged and short. Shown here in its starting state; in the Neuro-UX Lab every version and every stress test can be used.](_figures/d8_A3_0.png)

**Narrative communication and narratively structured guidance**

| Aspect | Narrative communication | Narratively structured guidance |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | A story about the product or brand. | A journey built like a story. |
| Where it lives | Films, campaigns, the website. | The first screen, transitions, confirmations. |
| Whose story | The company's. | The user's. |
| Its cost | Attention and time before the task. | A line or two inside the task. |

:::note Case assumption
Lantern Kitchens, Iris, Pavel, June and Arne are made up for this example. The dramatic arc follows Freytag (1863) and its use in design follows Lupton (2017); visibility of system status is Nielsen's heuristic (1994).
:::

:::rules How to decide when this comes up in the task
- Tension: does the user know what is at stake for them?
- Expectation: do they know what comes next, and roughly how much?
- Sequence: do the steps follow the user's logic, or the order of the database?
- Transition: does each step say what was done and lead to the next?
- Confirmation and emotional context: is a finished step acknowledged and framed by what it means for them?
:::

:::note Extra · go deeper (optional reading)
A good transition has three parts and fits in one line: a confirmation of what was done (“Your room is in.”), the expectation of what comes next (“Next: your style.”) and a word on its size or difficulty (“A few choices, all changeable.”). It is the cheapest piece of dramaturgy there is, and the one most often missing.
:::

^src: Lupton 2017 · Freytag 1863 · Nielsen 1994

## A4 · Orientation, motivation and memorability, and the risk of overload · 12 min · Core

> A clear story raises orientation, motivation and memorability, and makes people feel the product is for them. Too much staging, too little clarity, more text and longer paths turn it into overload that costs time and trust.

:::box In plain words
**The idea.** A clear story raises three things. Orientation: people know where they are and what comes next. Motivation: they see that they are getting closer. Memorability: they remember the journey and its end. Narrative clarity, emotional bond and perceived relevance go together: when people see why a step matters for them, they feel the product is for them. But storytelling can overload: too much staging, too little clarity, more text and longer paths. Then it costs efficiency and trust instead of building them.
**Why it matters.** Block 1.1 asks for StartSmart's two biggest weaknesses, Block 1.2 asks you to rate three journeys on orientation, motivation, relevance, efficiency and experience quality and to name the conflicts between efficiency and dramaturgy, and Block 2.1 warns against measures that are only staging. This card gives you what a story does and where it stops helping.
**How to read the picture.** The matrix places five Lantern Kitchens journeys by how much story they carry (upwards) and how clear and quick they stay (to the right). A position is a reading of the example, not a score.
:::

![Five Lantern Kitchens journeys by how much story they carry and how clear and quick they stay · Exploratory.](_figures/d8_A4_0.png)

**What a clear story does, and how it turns into overload**

| A clear story | What it raises | Overload instead |
|---|---|---|
| A goal and a path | Orientation: I know where I am. | A story that hides the steps. |
| Visible progress | Motivation: I am getting closer. | Long scenes between the steps. |
| A real ending | Memorability: I remember it worked. | A finale that delays the result. |
| The user's goal | Relevance and bond: this is for me. | The brand's story instead of mine. |

:::note Case assumption
Lantern Kitchens, Iris, Pavel, June and Arne are made up for this example. Being absorbed in a story and moved by it follows Green and Brock (2000); the goal-gradient effect follows Kivetz et al. (2006); not making people think follows Krug (2014).
:::

:::rules How to decide when this comes up in the task
- Check that every narrative element serves the task: does it orient, motivate or give context?
- Count what the story adds: screens, words, seconds. Is it worth it for these users?
- Prefer a goal, a path and short transitions to scenes and long texts.
- Make the story about the user's goal, not about the brand.
- If the steps are harder to find than before, the story has become overload.
:::

:::note Extra · go deeper (optional reading)
Green and Brock (2000) found that stories move people when they are absorbed in them, and that absorption depends on the story fitting the reader. In a product, users absorbed in a staged story about someone else's kitchen are not absorbed in their own task. The narrative that works in a journey is the user's own: their goal, their progress, their result.
:::

^src: Green & Brock 2000 · Kivetz et al. 2006 · Krug 2014

## A5 · Embedding an action in its context, and weighing a measure · 12 min · Core

> Storytelling activates, gives context and embeds an action in a meaning: a goal makes it easy to begin, a reason turns a demand into sense, a finished step framed as progress feels like achievement. Weigh measures on effect on users, feasibility, risk and time pressure.

:::box In plain words
**The idea.** Storytelling can activate people, give them context and embed what they do in a meaning. Activation: a goal and a next step make it easy to begin. Context: a line that says why a question is asked turns a demand into a reason. Emotional embedding: a finished step framed as progress towards their own goal feels like an achievement, not like paperwork. To put measures in order, weigh each on the four questions Block 2.1 names: effect on users, feasibility, risk and time pressure.
**Why it matters.** Block 1.2 asks for a decision between three journeys, and Block 2.1 asks for four measures, their justification on these four criteria and a decision for 12 weeks. The example uses Lantern Kitchens and its own measures, so the answer for the task cases is not given.
**How to read the picture.** First the chain from an action to its meaning for the user. Then three measures Lantern Kitchens considered, rated on effect on users, feasibility, risk and time pressure.
:::

![From an action to its meaning for the user.](_figures/d8_A5_0.png)

![Lantern Kitchens weighs three measures on effect on users, feasibility, risk and time pressure.](_figures/d8_A5_1.png)

:::note Case assumption
Lantern Kitchens, Iris, Pavel, June and Arne are made up for this example. Designing around users' goals follows Quesenbery and Brooks (2010); rating options on the same criteria with a reason follows Gibbons (2018). The four criteria are the ones the plan names for the case study.
:::

:::rules How to decide when this comes up in the task
- Activation: does the first screen give a goal and a clear first step?
- Context: does each demanding question say why it is asked?
- Emotional embedding: is each finished step framed as progress towards the user's goal?
- Rate every measure on the same four questions: effect on users, feasibility, risk, time pressure.
- A rating is a judgement with a reason, never a mark.
:::

:::note Extra · go deeper (optional reading)
Context turns a demand into a reason. “Enter your annual consumption” is a demand; “Your annual consumption shows which funding you can get” is the same field with a reason. The second needs a few more words, but it answers the question people ask silently at every field: why do they want this?
:::

^src: Quesenbery & Brooks 2010 · Kivetz et al. 2006 · Gibbons 2018

# Materi B · Route 2 · Level 3

:::note How Materi B is used
About 60 minutes, three cards, led by the facilitator. ## B1 · Narrative UX as experience architecture, not communication · 20 min · Core

> The journey people experience is built by product teams step by step, not by communication. Narrative UX designs it on purpose: a guiding vision, principles, patterns for entries, progress, transitions and endings, and an owner.

:::box In plain words
**The idea.** In many companies storytelling is a matter for communication: the brand story on the website, a campaign film. But the journey people experience is built by product teams, step by step, usually from the specialist logic. Narrative UX means designing that journey on purpose: a guiding vision for how every journey begins, carries people and ends, patterns for entries, progress, transitions and endings, and someone who owns them. At senior level it becomes a design logic for relevance, activation and bond, and part of the decision architecture.
**Why it matters.** Block 3.1 asks for a guiding vision for narrative UX, three decisions on entry, dramaturgical guidance, the experience of progress and narrative relevance, and an order of which journeys, touchpoints or interactions come first, for a company that sees storytelling as communication. You decide the journeys, not the texts.
**How to read the picture.** Left Lantern Kitchens treating storytelling as communication, right building it into the experience architecture. Below, the layers from a guiding vision to every step.
:::

![Lantern Kitchens treating storytelling as communication, or building it into the experience architecture.](_figures/d8_B1_0.png)

![The layers of narrative UX, from a guiding vision to every step.](_figures/d8_B1_1.png)

:::note Case assumption
Lantern Kitchens, Iris, Pavel, June and Arne are made up for this example. Storytelling as part of UX design follows Quesenbery and Brooks (2010) and Lupton (2017).
:::

:::rules How to decide when this comes up in the task
- Check where the story is decided today: in campaigns, or in the journeys themselves?
- Write a guiding vision that says how every journey begins, carries people and ends.
- Turn it into a few principles and patterns every team uses.
- Decide by journey which ones need most guidance: the long, important ones first.
- At senior level, storytelling is a design logic for relevance, activation and bond, and part of the decision architecture.
:::

:::note Extra · go deeper (optional reading)
A guiding vision for narrative UX is short and testable: one sentence that names the beginning, the way and the end. “In every journey customers know where it leads, see how far they are, and finish with something in hand” can be checked on every flow. “Every click tells a story” cannot, and it invites staging.
:::

^src: Quesenbery & Brooks 2010 · Lupton 2017 · Bruner 1991

## B2 · Efficiency against story: the conflicts of narrative UX · 20 min · Core

> Efficiency against dramaturgy, emotionalisation against seriousness, comprehensibility against staging, brand effect against the user's task. Use story only where it serves the task, keep it short, and decide by journey.

:::box In plain words
**The idea.** Narrative UX sits between goals that pull against each other: efficiency against dramaturgy (people with little time against a journey with moments), emotionalisation against seriousness (inspiring against credible), comprehensibility against staging (plain steps against a story), and brand effect against the user's task (what marketing wants to tell against what the user came to do). The way through is to use story only where it serves the task, keep it short, and decide by journey: a long, important journey needs more guidance than a quick routine one.
**Why it matters.** Block 3.1 asks for three guiding decisions, a risk analysis of wrong assumptions about user needs, storytelling and seriousness, and a governance model for conflicts between clarity, brand and experience quality. Each of these is a decision between conflicting goals.
**How to read the picture.** Three Lantern Kitchens journeys of different length and weight, with how much narrative guidance each needs.
:::

![A Lantern Kitchens reorder with three amounts of narrative guidance. Shown here in its starting state; in the Neuro-UX Lab every version and every stress test can be used.](_figures/d8_B2_0.png)

**Standing conflicts of narrative UX**

| Conflict | Who usually stands for each side | What to ask |
|---|---|---|
| Efficiency against dramaturgy | Users with little time, against a journey with moments. | How long and how important is this journey for them? |
| Emotionalisation against seriousness | Marketing, against the specialist department. | Can it be inspiring and correct in the same line? |
| Brand effect against the user's task | The brand story, against what the user came to do. | Whose story is this step telling? |

:::note Case assumption
Lantern Kitchens, Iris, Pavel, June and Arne are made up for this example. The three journeys are a reading of the example, not a rule for every product.
:::

:::rules How to decide when this comes up in the task
- Name the conflict as two goals and say who stands for each: marketing, the department, the product team, the users.
- Decide by journey: a quick routine needs no story, a long and important one needs a goal, a path and an ending.
- Treat every staged scene and long text inside a flow as a cost to efficiency and clarity, and ask what it buys.
- Write the rule down, so the conflict is not fought again in every flow.
- Say what the choice gives up; a decision that gives up nothing has not been made.
:::

:::note Extra · go deeper (optional reading)
A simple rule settles most conflicts: the amount of narrative guidance grows with the length of the journey and its weight for the user. A rule like this, written into the principles, lets marketing tell its story where people have time for it (the website) and keeps it out of the steps where they do not.
:::

^src: Green & Brock 2000 · Krug 2014 · Gibbons 2018

## B3 · Who owns narrative UX, and deciding without seeing where people stop · 20 min · Core

> Narrative UX lives only with governance: an owner of the principles and patterns, experts who check the facts, marketing who owns the brand story, and a rule for conflicts. A decision with little data says what it decides, does not know, would reverse and gives up.

:::box In plain words
**The idea.** Narrative UX lives only with governance: someone who owns the principles and the patterns, the experts who check that the story never blurs the facts, marketing who owns the brand story, and a rule for when they disagree. Data on journeys is often incomplete; people cannot be seen where they stop. A decision under uncertainty says what you decide, what you do not know, when you would reverse it and what you give up. An entry, a progress display and a transition are cheap to change and quick to check, which makes them a safe start.
**Why it matters.** Block 3.1 asks for a governance model for narrative UX principles, a decision despite patchy data, and a statement to the management. This card gives you the parts of each.
**How to read the picture.** The four boxes at the top are the parts of a decision, filled in for Lantern Kitchens. The two boxes below are the rule for future decisions.
:::

![A decision under uncertainty, filled in for Lantern Kitchens.](_figures/d8_B3_0.png)

**Three parts of narrative UX governance**

| Part | The question | An example (Lantern Kitchens) |
|---|---|---|
| Who owns what | Who owns the principles and patterns; who uses them? | The head of customer experience owns them; every team uses them. |
| Who checks | Who makes sure the story never blurs the facts? | The planning experts check every line; marketing checks the brand. |
| How a conflict is decided | What happens when clarity, brand and experience quality disagree? | Clarity, then the customer, then the brand; the product owner settles a tie. |

:::note Case assumption
Lantern Kitchens, Iris, Pavel, June and Arne are made up for this example. Deciding with limited information follows Simon (1956); the premortem follows Klein (2007).
:::

:::rules How to decide when this comes up in the task
- Name who owns the narrative UX principles and patterns, and who only uses them.
- Give the specialist department a veto on clarity, and marketing ownership of the brand story.
- Settle conflicts by clarity first, then the user's experience in that step, then the brand.
- Say what you decide, what you do not know, when you would reverse it and what you give up.
- Start where changes are cheap, quick to check and easy to reverse: entries, progress, transitions.
:::

:::note Extra · go deeper (optional reading)
Imagine it is a year later and the narrative journeys have failed, and ask why (Klein 2007). The usual answers: the story grew in every release until the steps were hard to find; marketing used the patterns for campaigns; the experts stopped checking the lines. Each answer is a governance gap, not a writing gap.
:::

^src: Simon 1956 · Klein 2007 · Quesenbery & Brooks 2010
