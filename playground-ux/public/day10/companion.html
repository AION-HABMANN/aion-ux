Designing how AI feels, not how it works

This day is about one question: when does AI inside an interface actually help a person — and when does it quietly take control, hide its reasoning, or break their trust? You'll answer it by using nine real, free AI tools and auditing their UX.

Start here

The one thing this whole day is about

Netflix knows you like thrillers, yet its recommendations still feel off sometimes. Google Maps reroutes you without asking. A chatbot feels brilliant one moment and frustrating the next. Behind every one of these moments is a design decision — not just an engineering one.

TransparencyCan the user see why the system did what it did?
ControlCan the user change, steer, or override it?
RecoverabilityIf the AI gets it wrong, can it be undone?
The test you'll use all day: If the answer to all three is "no", the feature needs a redesign before it ships — no matter how clever the AI behind it is.

The pattern to watch
Notice how each category solves transparency & control differently. Research AI leans hard on citations (visible transparency). Chat AI leans on conversation (control through dialogue). Builder AI trades control for speed. Creative AI is often the most opaque — great output, little explanation. Keep asking: could the user tell why, and could they change it?

Task 1 · Level 1 Direct observation ▸

Goal: run the same prompt in Claude, Gemini and NotebookLM. Record what each one shows you vs hides. There's an objective right/wrong on the system-type column.

Prepared prompt (copy exactly into Claude and Gemini):
"I want you to help me plan a birthday party for my friend. Before you give me any plan, ask me whatever you need to know to make it fit my friend — do not assume the details yourself."

The point: identical instruction, but Claude and Gemini often behave differently. That contrast is the lesson. For NotebookLM, upload the ai-ux-primer.pdf and watch for clickable citations.

Task 2 · Level 1 Classify the system ▸

Goal: for each feature, tick one system type and justify it in a line. Exactly one label is correct per row.

The decision tree — ask these in order:

  1. Does the same input always give the same output, with fixed logic? → Rule-based
  2. Does it adjust to your current context but forget you next time? → Adaptive
  3. Does it improve from accumulated data and can vary over time? → Learning
The intended trap: "v0's default colours before you edit." The whole tool uses a learned model — so people say "learning". But the defaults themselves are fixed design tokens applied identically every time. Correct answer: rule-based. Separate the fixed template from the learned generation.

Task 3 · Level 2 Deep AI-UX audit ▸

Goal: pick ONE tool (Bolt, Lovable, Dremina, Perplexity), audit one feature against the three lenses, then propose a fix. Graded by rubric — every claim needs a screenshot.

The three lenses, as audit questions:

Transparency

Does it explain why it chose this? Can you see the reasoning or sources?

Control

Can you override or steer it? How much friction to change one thing?

Recoverability

If it's wrong, can you undo or regenerate? Is anything lost?

The consistency rule that earns marks: your Step D redesign must fix the exact weakness you named in Step C. If you say "transparency is weakest" then propose a control fix, you lose points even with a beautiful sketch.

Worked mini-audit — Perplexity: Transparency = high (cites nearly everything). Weakness = it cites confidently even from weak sources. Redesign = add a source-quality badge (forum vs peer-reviewed) so users can judge citation strength. Notice the redesign targets the named weakness.

Task 4 · Level 3 The strategic call ▸

Goal: you're the CXO of "BelajarCepat". Pick ONE AI direction to build this quarter under hard constraints. Graded on internal consistency — two people can pick opposite directions and both score 100.

What the constraints quietly reward:

DirectionFit under thin data + fragile trust + ship this quarter
A · Clarifying assistant (Claude-style)Strong — needs little data, low trust risk
B · Cited answers (Perplexity-style)Strong — builds trust, no personal data
C · Auto-generated plans (Bolt-style)Viable if paired with easy override
D · Deep personalised feedWeakest — needs data + consent you lack
The master rule: your scores, your decision, your risk analysis and your statement to the CEO must all point the same way. You can still pick D and score full marks — but only if you honestly address the data/consent problem. Picking D while ignoring thin data is the classic inconsistency.
